# Results — reading the numbers

Generated by `src/evaluate.py` over 40 labeled cases (`data/cases.jsonl`,
deterministic seed 20260830). Labels are hidden at decision time and revealed only
for scoring. Raw numbers: `metrics.json`; per-decision audit rows:
`decisions_<policy>.jsonl`; table: `report.md`.

## The three deciders

- **full** — the section-6 policy: belief + expected loss + BUY (capped at 2) + FLAG.
- **autonomous** — same belief + expected loss, but **no human and no buy**; must
  commit to ACCEPT / REPAIR / REJECT (the student's Policy B).
- **baseline** — a "dumb" calibrated **score + threshold** policy (ACCEPT if
  P(correct) ≥ 0.90, REJECT if ≤ 0.40, else FLAG). It ignores *which* failure world,
  so it can never REPAIR or pick a targeted next probe. This is the exact strawman a
  practitioner (PLBjt, r/learnmachinelearning) said to build as the honest comparison.

## Headline findings

| policy | avg cost | human-review rate | wrong publishes (cost 20) |
|--------|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| full | **2.30** | 0.575 | 2 |
| autonomous | 3.08 | 0.00 | 2 |
| baseline | 3.10 | 0.33 | 2 |

**Read all of this as preliminary: N = 40, no confidence intervals.** A 0.8-cost gap
on 40 cases is indicative, not a proven win (preprint review, Sec 11).

1. **The full belief policy has the lowest *average* decision cost** (2.30 vs ~3.1)
   on this sample — but see finding 3: most of that gap comes from escalating to a
   human, not from making better autonomous calls. The belief layer **changes**
   decisions; the data here do **not** yet prove it makes *better* ones.
2. **Rent test (PLBjt):** the full policy agrees with the dumb baseline on only
   **62.5%** of cases — far below the ~95% "not paying rent" line. That shows the
   belief layer *differs* from score+threshold; on its own, divergence is **not**
   evidence it's better (preprint review). The fair test — comparing at a **fixed
   human-review budget**, or showing **fewer worst-case errors** — is future work.
   **Significance (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank, added after the AI probability
   review):** the cost gap *is* significant (full vs baseline **p ≈ 0.015**; full vs
   autonomous **p ≈ 0.003**) — but the **median per-case cost difference is 0.0**, so
   most cases *tie* and the mean gap comes from a minority of cheaper mid-cases, not a
   broad per-case win. Real, but narrow — and it does not touch the 2/2/2 catastrophes.
   (Normal approximation, N=40: indicative, not definitive; see `report.md`.)
3. **The lower cost is largely bought by escalation.** The full policy flags **57.5%**
   of cases vs. the baseline's ~33%. Holding review rate fixed is the honest
   comparison; tuning the ACCEPT threshold / VOI rule to cut flagging is clear future
   work. And FLAG is modelled as a flat cost 2 that *perfectly* resolves — a real,
   fallible, capacity-limited human would raise the true cost of this heavy escalation
   (decision model §8.2 open-Q8).
4. **All three eat the 2 deliberate failure seeds** (cost-20 wrong publishes). These
   are the F1/F2 `wrong_product` cases that look normal and even have cross-source
   agreement — no policy catches them without a new **internal-consistency** signal
   (the exact fix the r/WebScrapingInsider discussion suggested). The experiment
   *demonstrates* the documented blind spot rather than hiding it.

## Calibration (reliability by confidence bin)

For the full policy, the top confidence bin (P(correct) 0.95–1.01) was only ~60%
empirically correct (3/5 cases). This is **not "slightly" overconfident — it is a
severe miscalibration** (preprint review, Sec 11): the model claims ≥95% but is right
~60% of the time, exactly where a wrong ACCEPT costs 20. **It breaks the expected-loss
math:** the *true* expected loss of ACCEPT in that bin is ≈ 0.40 × 20 = **8**, well
above FLAG (2) and REJECT (5) — so on these numbers "ACCEPT at ≥0.95" is actually the
*worse* action, and the §6 threshold derivation only holds once the posterior is
calibrated. **Consequence:** the 95% ACCEPT threshold is a *design target*, not a
validated operational risk level — it must not be read as "95% safe" until the ≥0.95
bin is shown to hit high precision on an independently audited sample, after which the
threshold must be re-derived from the *calibrated* probabilities. (Caveat: "60%" is
3/5 on 40 cases — direction is clear, magnitude is not statistically settled.) (autonomous and baseline share one calibration table
because, with no buys, their beliefs are identical — only the *action mapping*
differs.)

## Known limitation — evaluation bias (active learning)

Raised by a practitioner (PaddingCompression, r/learnmachinelearning): this is an
**active-learning** setup, so the evaluation is biased. The agent auto-accepts a
large bucket it never verifies and only the flagged bucket is human-checked, so
scoring across our labeled set over-weights the ambiguous cases. The honest fix
(future work) is to **randomly audit a slice of the auto-accepted bucket** and
**importance-weight** it back to estimate true population accuracy/calibration. Read
these numbers with that caveat. (See §8 open question 7 in the decision model.)

## Reproduce

```bash
cd src
python3 generate_data.py   # writes ../data/cases.jsonl (deterministic)
python3 evaluate.py        # writes ../results/{metrics.json,report.md,decisions_*.jsonl}
```

Pure standard library (Python 3.11); no third-party dependencies. Every prior,
likelihood, and cost traces to sections 3–6 of
`decisions/belief-engine-decision-model.md`.
